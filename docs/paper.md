Designing for the Reviewer: A Human-in-the-Loop AI Pipeline for News-Driven Authority File Maintenance at the National Library and Archives Board Singapore[A(1.1]
Ashwin Nair
Abstract
Keeping authority files current from the news is a solved problem in principle and an unsolved one in practice: the reading never stops, and few articles matter. This paper reports the building of an interface at the National Library and Archives Board Singapore that reads the news and proposes updates to its name authority file; run over 298,790 articles from 2015 to 2026, it generated candidate changes to existing records for 9,844 entities, none yet reviewed. Its lessons come from cases met along the way, not from controlled tests. The first is that a proposal a person cannot judge is not output: the first version produced over nine thousand that no one could act on. The second is that the failures that mattered were found by someone looking at the output, not by a test, which argues for building the review interface first. The third is to check first how much of the work comes from the news: a rough check of the file's history suggests most of it does not. The evaluation has not yet run.
1. The problem
When someone searches a library catalogue for Lee Kuan Yew, they should find everything the library holds about him, including material catalogued under his Chinese name, 李光耀, or under variant romanizations. An authority file makes this work. Each authority record establishes the preferred form of a name, records its variants, and carries structured fields such as occupation, affiliation, dates of birth and death, and a short description written by a cataloguer. Catalogue records link to it, and a search expands across every variant it holds; without it, a search retrieves only what matches the exact string typed.
The National Library and Archives Board’s Thesaurus and Taxonomy Editor (TTE) holds the Board’s name authority file: 52,437 records covering persons, organizations, buildings, places, events, awards, programmes and legal acts. These are the name-authority part of a file of about 1.8 million records that also holds subject headings and other vocabularies. TTE’s records are the data dictionary behind the Board’s discovery systems and the National Archives’ Archives Online; other government agencies draw on its names for their own indexing, and selected records are published on VIAF, the international linked data network for authority data. A record that is wrong or out of date does not stay within the Board’s catalogue; it propagates through every system that depends on it.
Keeping these records current is routine cataloguing work: a person’s record needs a new affiliation when they take up a post and a death date when they die, and an organization's needs updating when it merges or is renamed. What makes it hard to sustain is volume, not neglect. The knowledge organization team’s practice has been to read the news: specialists monitor the major Singapore outlets, in English and Chinese, and update records by hand. The feeds of the three outlets this project follows carry about 60 articles a day between them, and fewer than one in ten bears on an authority record. Reading all of them, every day, for the few that matter is more than a small team can sustain, and it wears down attention long before it produces an obvious error; each added source or language makes it worse. The same volume problem drove earlier automation in authority maintenance, such as event-triggered data feeds that updated death dates in bibliographic records at scale (Kreyche, Lisius & Park, 2010).
The question this work set out to answer was how an AI-assisted authority pipeline should be designed so that its proposals stay reviewable, auditable and safe for cataloguers. The literature on AI in library cataloguing is optimistic but thin on specifics: a review of 25 papers found only 8 that described actual integration workflows, multilingual applications underexplored, and nearly half naming no cataloguing standard or controlled vocabulary, and it identified authority work as a gap still to be examined in depth (Engel et al., 2025). Four of the papers said human review of AI-produced records is necessary, but little about what that review should look like. This paper describes a complete workflow for authority work, in English and Chinese, held to TTE's exact field schema, and is centrally concerned with what the review should look like. The contribution is the design and the failure cases, and evaluation is planned but has not yet run.
2. The system
The pipeline has six stages. The first five run unattended, on a schedule. Only the last involves a person.
Ingestion collects article metadata from the RSS feeds of three Singapore news outlets in English and Chinese. Only the articles that pass relevance are fetched in full, and the text is not kept: the pipeline stores what it extracts, not what it reads. A page that cannot be read is recorded and given up after three attempts.
Relevance asks: does this article describe a change that would affect an authority record? A single model call scores up to a hundred articles at once from title and summary alone, returning only those that pass. The kinds of change it looks for are: a person appointed, retired, honored or deceased; an organization founded, merged or renamed; a building opened or given heritage status. Between 3 August and 28 September 2026, 149 of the 3,511 (~4%) articles collected passed.
Extraction asks: who is mentioned, and what does the article say about them? Each relevant article is read in full. A model call returns the entities mentioned and the facts the story supports about each, under TTE’s own field names, with one sentence quoted verbatim as evidence so a reviewer can check the source.
Retrieval asks: which authority records might this entity be? This stage uses no model. It searches the database using character-level similarity (trigrams) on both the name as written and its English romanization. This is fast and tolerates minor spelling variation. An embeddings-based alternative was tried in a small pilot and dropped: it scored two different people as more similar to each other than one person’s name written in two scripts, which would rank wrong candidates above correct ones. Retrieval methods were not compared systematically. The Library of Congress’s experiments, which included vector search for matching field values to authority records, found accuracy below 50 per cent on subject and genre (Saccucci & Potter, 2025). The stage returns a shortlist of candidates. One deliberate exception to the recall-early principle in section 3 applies here: a person recorded as dead more than a year before the article is removed, unless the article reports a death or is a memorial or posthumous piece, on the working assumption that such a person is rarely the subject of a news story. It is a hard rule, so a retrospective, anniversary or archive piece that fits none of those exceptions would lose a correct candidate; showing excluded candidates to the reviewer would avoid that.
Resolution asks: is this the same entity, and if so, what does the record lack? This is the only stage that sees both the article and the authority record at once. A model call receives each extracted entity alongside its candidates and their fields, and is asked those two questions in order. The prompt is explicit that a name match is not an identity match, two people can share a name, and the model must use the structured fields to decide.
Review presents the proposals to a human, one decision at a time. Proposals about the same record are merged first, so multiple articles about one person become one identity decision. The reviewer sees the identity question first, then each proposed change one screen at a time. If no candidate fits, the reviewer can search the full authority file directly. Approved changes leave as an Excel file in a layout the team agreed, and the reviewer makes each one in TTE and marks it done. The pipeline never writes to the authority file directly. This separation between an automated layer that feeds proposals and a human layer that applies them follows the same logic as earlier authority maintenance projects that used shadow files, separate automated records used solely for matching, never written to the main file (Kreyche, Lisius & Park, 2010).
The same prompts have also been run over the three outlets’ archives back to 2015, amounting to 295,279 articles, of which 27,941 were judged relevant. Extraction returned usable output for 12,447 of these (44.5 per cent, after two rounds), naming 48,387 entities; why the rest failed is taken up below. Across archive and live feed together, resolution proposed a change to an existing record for 9,844 entities, none yet reviewed, and found 22,331 entities with no record at all.
To make this concrete: on 31 August 2026, The Straits Times reported that a social service veteran had stepped down as chairperson of the Agency for Integrated Care. The article passed relevance with the reason “Gerard Ee stepped down and Anita Fam was appointed as chairperson.” Extraction returned two entities, the outgoing chairperson and the incoming one, each with an occupation, a description and a quoted source sentence. Retrieval found the correct record for Gerard Ee and one other, which was far below the best match to be dropped automatically. Resolution confirmed the match and proposed two updates: a new affiliation, and a single clause added to the description that left 99 per cent of the cataloguer’s text untouched. The reviewer saw one identity question and two field screens.
3. The handoff problem
An early version of the pipeline processed a backlog of news articles and surfaced over nine thousand candidate updates. The output was a flat list of entity names and proposed changes, with no source sentence to check a change against and no confirmation of whose record it was. A librarian examining it could not make a single decision.
The lesson was that a proposal a reviewer cannot judge is not output, it is work moved from the machine to the person and left there. If the interface is not usable, people will not use it, and the pipeline produces nothing however good the backend is. It is also worse than unusable: people given AI suggestions tend to settle on a general sense of whether to trust the system rather than check each one (Buçinca, Malaya & Gajos, 2021), and a list that offers nothing to check invites exactly that. The Library of Congress’s cataloguing experiments found model output short of the quality needed to run unattended, around 80 per cent accurate across fields and below 50 per cent on subject and genre, and named human-in-the-loop workflows as the next step (Saccucci & Potter, 2025). Every subsequent design decision in this project was made against a single standard: can a person decide this in seconds?
Four principles shaped what followed. The system never writes to the authority file, and every proposal ends at a named person who is accountable for it. Recall early, precision late: a news story wrongly judged irrelevant at the first stage is permanently and silently lost, while a wrong match at the last stage is a proposal a reviewer rejects in seconds, so errors belong at the end where they are visible and cheap (with one exception, the death-date filter in section 2). The prompt asks; the code checks: the model is told the field names it should use, but code enforces them after it answers, because a prompt is a request and code is a rule. And one question per screen: the review interface presents decisions as a sequence, first identity, then one proposed change at a time, so nothing requires the reviewer to remember anything from the previous screen. It aims at what Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021) call cognitive forcing, deliberate engagement with each proposal, but is weaker than the designs they tested: the proposal is shown at once, and nothing stops a reviewer approving on sight.
4. What the failures taught us
Most of the following failures became visible only because the interface made someone look carefully at the output; the others were found by measuring it.
Identity lives in facts, not names. A name alone cannot tell you which of several people a news article means; the other information on the record, job title, organisation, birth year, can. A name match is a lead, not a verdict, and that is true exactly where it is most tempting to skip the check: when the name has been translated by the model itself. Matching Chinese names a second time through their English romanisation raised the match rate from 40 to 77 per cent, but the romanisation is a guess. Of 66 Chinese-named people in the feed, TTE held the Chinese name for only 17; for the rest, the guessed spelling was the only bridge to the file. One guess landed exactly on a different man’s record, a banker who died in 1966, matched at perfect name similarity. The resolution stage rejected the match on his death year and flagged the entity instead of confirming it; the reviewer found the right record by search. The fix: search the original-language name first, treat any AI-guessed romanisation as weaker evidence, and mark it visibly to the reviewer. Each Chinese name a reviewer confirms against an English record is now also proposed as a variant name for the cataloguer to add, so the file’s own coverage of Chinese names grows with every case it corrects.
Editing a description is not writing one. Good advice for writing a description from scratch (such as “keep it short, don’t list every post held”) becomes actively harmful applied to an existing one: it tells the model to delete things that were already there. It did exactly that, more than once, cutting a Navy career, ten years as Deputy Prime Minister and every constituency held from a 636-character description, reducing it to 405 characters and calling the result a merge. The danger is not a loud error but a quiet one: nobody would notice unless they compared the old and new text side by side, and a library cannot afford to silently lose a real historical fact. The fix separates writing from editing in the guidance the model is given and measures every rewrite for how much of the original text survives. The standard text similarity measure penalises addition as much as deletion, and flagged rewrites that had deleted nothing at all; the useful measure counts matched characters against the length of the old text alone, since deletion is what a reviewer needs to be warned about. Rewrites that delete heavily now reach the reviewer with a visible warning.
A match with nothing to change wastes attention. In the first batch, 16 of 49 matches proposed no change, because the authority file already held every fact the article reported. Shown as a confirmation button over an empty space, they read as a broken page; shown with the values the record already held, they at least read as an answer. The first reviewer set every one aside as a waste of attention anyway, and the deck was rebuilt on that verdict: a record with nothing to decide is no longer dealt as a card, and is listed at the end so the reviewer can see the article was read.
The schema belongs in the code, not the prompt. TTE’s guidelines define 113 fields with exact names, permitted values and exclusions. When the pipeline used its own field names instead, values were silently filed under wrong fields: a value for the affiliation field created a new field rather than extending the real one, because the stored field name includes a suffix the pipeline had omitted. Enforcing field names and controlled vocabularies in code after the model answers eliminated this class of error in the live runs observed, though it does not stop a model choosing the wrong valid field. Injecting all 34 kilobytes of cataloguing guidance into the prompt made the model skim it, use short field names anyway, and find fewer entities per article. A longer prompt was a worse one. The archive showed the cost of asking without enforcing. Its internal model cannot be held to a schema, and in the first round extraction returned the requested structure for 8,149 of 22,996 articles; the rest came back as prose, as the input handed back, or without the evidence sentence a reviewer needs. The live pipeline’s model API enforces the schema, and it has not failed this way. Yang et al. (2026) report the same in clinical documentation: under a free-text prompt one model configuration produced valid output for only 74–79 per cent of notes per run, while under API-level schema enforcement all three models tested were valid in every run.
5. Scope of automation: the news-addressable ceiling
Before evaluating the pipeline, it helps to know what share of the team's work it can reach. Comparing seven TTE exports from September 2024 to September 2026 gives the team’s own edit history: 4,046 field changes. For about one in eight, one of the three outlets printed the record’s name in a headline or summary in that period or the two months before; a full-text check on the articles where full text was available found 1.6 times as many names, which would put the figure nearer one in five. The rest comes from sources this pipeline cannot read. The evaluation will therefore report two figures: this ceiling, and the pipeline’s recall within it.
In a rough check of the changes the pipeline could in principle have reached, it had already picked up about three in ten before the team made them. About four in ten mentioned the name only in passing. About three in ten were real misses.
A printed name is necessary but not sufficient: an article can mention a name without reporting the change, and a name in a different romanization is missed entirely. These figures are estimates of a ceiling, not measurements of failure. No model can raise that ceiling, since none can propose a change about a name it never saw. More sources raise part of it. The rest is cataloguing work with no news behind it at all.
6. What we don’t know yet
The evaluation has not yet run. Three labelled sheets are drawn from one month of the feed: every article the pipeline judged relevant plus a stratified sample of what it rejected; matched and rejected identity pairs; and articles checked against what was extracted from them. Labelers do not see the pipeline’s answer, fifty rows of each sheet are marked twice so inter-rater agreement can be reported as Cohen’s kappa, and labels are frozen before scoring. Alongside it, two cataloguers use the application while two read the news as before, crossing over at six weeks so each person serves as their own control on time per change. Relevance and extraction are scored as precision, recall and F1, and identity matching as match precision and recall, split by Latin- and Chinese-script names; the crossover compares time per change. The proposals are decidable. Whether they are correct is still to be measured.
Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021) found that interfaces designed to make people engage carefully with each AI suggestion reduced overreliance, but were also the designs people liked least. The review desk in this pipeline asks for that kind of engagement. Whether it stays acceptable when a cataloguer is working through fifty proposals in a session rather than five is not yet known.
7. What transfers
The pipeline was built by one person and runs on the free tiers of a hosted database, a scheduled task runner and the Gemini API, using a chain of models led by gemini-3.8-flash with thinking set to high, with lighter models answering when a daily allowance is spent. Paid API access, which would remove those quotas, is estimated at under SGD 10 a month at current volume, excluding staff time, development effort and the platform used for the archive. What is costly is the combination of skills required: someone or a team who can think across entity resolution, ML system design, library metadata practice, and interface design simultaneously. Institutions attempting this should plan for that requirement honestly.
Beyond the right person, the requirements are an export of the authority file with its field structure, the cataloguing rules treated as a schema rather than as guidance, and a news source with a feed. Two constraints also need settling first. The pipeline copies an article only to analyze it: the page is fetched, entities and a single evidence sentence are extracted, and the body is discarded without being written to the database. This is designed to fall within Singapore’s computational data analysis exception (Copyright Act 2021, ss. 243–244); a team in another jurisdiction should check the equivalent provision first. And the system asserts facts about living people into a file that other agencies and VIAF draw on. The control is procedural: nothing reaches the authority file without a named cataloguer’s decision, which is why the accountability principle is not merely good practice but the actual safeguard.
In this project, the improvements we observed came from changes in code and in the shape of the question put to the reviewer, not from changing the model, and the clearest failure, the archive’s unstructured answers, was one of enforcement rather than capability. Models were not compared directly, so this is not a claim that the model does not matter.
We expect these design principles to transfer, though that is untested: never write to the authority file; enforce the schema in code after the answer, not in the prompt that requests it; keep errors at the end where they are visible and cheap; ask identity before update. One matters most in our experience: build the review interface before you trust the pipeline. In this project, displaying description changes as a diff, unchanged text in grey, additions highlighted, deletions struck through in red, revealed a deletion problem the pipeline had been producing for weeks. Laying out what each matched record already held revealed that about a third of the proposals proposed nothing. In both cases the pipeline had been wrong for a while; the interface was what made it visible. A system whose output cannot be examined clearly cannot be improved.
8. Conclusion
Keeping an authority file current from the news presents three distinct problems: relevance, identity and editing. They occur in that order, but their costs differ. Relevance is fast and cheap, but a story wrongly dropped there is lost for good, so it is where a miss costs most, and how many it misses has not yet been measured. Identity is an expensive judgement, and the design gives the model its best chance, thereby keeping the context small and filtering the candidates. Editing is where the least care does the most damage, because a description deleted is harder to recover than a description never written before.
The point is not to hand decisions to AI, even in places AI could plausibly make them. It is to cut the steps and the information a person needs to decide, find the record, gather the evidence, draft the change, so the decision stays fast enough to happen at all, without ever leaving that person’s hands. The institution remains accountable for the authority data it publishes; the workflow exists to keep an identifiable human decision before any AI-generated proposal can change it.
Keeping a person in that loop only works if the tool is one they would want to use: accurate, but also fast to move through and not cognitively exhausting. The desk has small touches built for exactly that, such as a visible stamp as each decision lands and a line marking progress every ten records, and nothing that rewards speed over judgement: no countdown, no score. In formative testing, two cataloguers used it, told us the first version was wrong, and helped redesign it.
